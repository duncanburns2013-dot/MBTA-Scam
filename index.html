Less than half of every MBTA dollar goes to actually moving people. The rest goes to debt (much of it from the Big Dig), pension shortfalls, and overhead — none of which were caused by transit workers.

📋 What Caused This?

In 2000, the state passed “Forward Funding” — transferring financial responsibility for the MBTA from the state general fund to a dedicated sales tax stream. The promise: 1% of state sales tax revenue would grow enough to fund the system. The reality: sales tax revenue has chronically underperformed, creating a cumulative shortfall of $9–15 billion. Simultaneously, the state dumped $8 billion+ of Big Dig highway debt onto the transit agency’s books — debt for a highway project that had nothing to do with trains or buses.

The Big Dig Debt Scam

In 2000, Massachusetts dumped $8 billion+ in highway debt onto a transit agency. The MBTA has been paying for roads it doesn’t use ever since.

The Central Artery/Tunnel Project (“Big Dig”) was a highway project. It had nothing to do with trains or buses. But when the state restructured MBTA finances in 2000, it transferred billions in Big Dig-related debt to the transit authority’s books.

🔥 The Bottom Line

This crisis was not caused by greedy workers, generous pensions, or riders who don’t pay enough. It was caused by 25 years of deliberate policy choices: dumping highway debt on a transit agency, promising funding that was never delivered, hiring $610/hour consultants instead of $35/hour bus drivers, and kicking the can down the road until the system broke. The money to fix it exists — $1.1 billion per year without any new taxes. What’s missing is the political courage to take it back from the people who are profiting from the dysfunction.
